The first thing I nearly accomplished at UBC was leaving it. In October of my first year, all of my midterms came back between 50 and 60 percent, and I was looking up flights home, how to withdraw from my degree, and how much money I had wasted coming here. By November, I had lost 25 pounds, and every day was filled with terror. I didn't yet know that what I was fighting had a name---Generalized Anxiety Disorder.

With treatment and support from the Centre for Accessibility, I began to recover, but mentors helped me find my footing. Three years later, I am one of eight students in the Honours Economics program, and I have tried to become the same mentors that supported me in first year. As Editor-in-Chief of IONA, UBC's undergraduate economics journal, I teach over 40 junior students how to critically analyze economic research. I am launching a graduate school mentorship program for women in economics and the social sciences. I also mentor with AGORA and the Undergraduate Research Opportunities club, and volunteer as a peer tutor at the VSE.

% worked on redesigning econ to be ml forward
Late in my first and second year, I got my first taste of research helping redesign UBC's economics curriculum around the machine learning tools social scientists now use. Concurrently, I joined a project on how Canada's public health officials were treated online during COVID-19, where we found that trusted experts were driven out of public conversation, with misinformation filling that gap.

% In my third year, my team won the Bank of Canada's national Governor's Challenge, presenting our forecast of the Canadian economy to the Bank's leadership, only the second win in UBC's history.

In my third year, my team took home the second win of the Bank of Canada Governor's Challenge for UBC. The Bank then hired me as one of three summer researchers. Canada's mortgage rules decide which families can buy a home, meaning policymakers need to balance protecting families and access to housing. To help the Bank predict the effects of changes in mortgage rules, I measured the effects of past ones by having a computer read decades of financial news. I presented this work to economists across the Bank, and it now forms the basis of a paper with two Bank economists.

My Honours Thesis uses game theory to tackle one of the most pressing problems in the next few decades---the problem of AI alignment. AI systems are quickly taking on high-consequence work, where a system pursuing even slightly wrong goals can cause irreversible harm, making alignment with human intent important. If AI systems can tell when they are being tested, our safety tests tell us very little. Game theory, the mathematics of strategic decisions, was built for this kind of problem. My thesis applies game theory to AI. I treat the developer and the AI system as two players, and work out how often, and in what form, developers should run surprise tests so that behaving well at all times becomes the AI's best strategy. 

Next, I plan to complete a Master's in Computer Science at UBC and then a PhD in economics, with the goal of furthering this alignment research.
