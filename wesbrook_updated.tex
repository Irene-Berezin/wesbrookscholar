The first thing I nearly accomplished at UBC was leaving it. In October of my first year, all of my midterms came back between 50 and 60 percent, and I was looking up flights home, how to withdraw from my degree, and how much money I had wasted coming here. By November, I had a withdrawal on my transcript, I had lost 25 pounds, and every day was filled with terror. I didn't yet know that what I was fighting had a name--- Generalized Anxiety Disorder.

With treatment, support from the Centre for Accessibility, and remarkable professors, I found my footing. Three years later, I am one of eight students in the Honours Economics program, and I have tried to become the support I didn't have in first year. As Editor-in-Chief of IONA, UBC's premier undergraduate economics journal, I teach 40 first- to third-year students statistics and how to critically analyze economic research. I am launching a graduate school mentorship program for women in economics and the social sciences. I also mentor with AGORA and the Undergraduate Research Opportunities club, and volunteer as a peer tutor at the VSE.

In my second year, I joined a UBC project on how Canada's public health officials were treated online during COVID-19. While colleagues interviewed health experts, nearly all of whom had been abused online, I analyzed 8 million tweets and uncovered large-scale harassment campaigns against public health officials. We found that trusted experts were driven out of public conversation, with misinformation filling that gap. 

In my third year, my team won the Bank of Canada's national Governor's Challenge, presenting our forecast of the Canadian economy to the Bank's leadership, only the second win in UBC's history. The Bank then hired me as one of three undergraduate summer researchers. Canada's mortgage rules, such as the mortgage stress test, decide which families can buy a home. I built a new way to measure their effects by having a computer read decades of Canadian financial news. I presented this work to economists across the Bank, and it now forms the basis of a paper with two Bank economists. Work like this helps our Central Bank judge whether its rules actually protect families, without shutting them out of owning a home. I also received the Bank's \$10,000 Master's Scholarship Award for Women in Economics and Finance.

My Honours thesis uses game theory to tackle one of the most pressing problems in AI safety--- the problem of AI alignment. If AI systems can tell when they are being tested, our safety tests tell us very little. Game theory, the mathematics of strategic decisions, was built for this kind of problem: economists use it to design random audits that keep companies honest between inspections. With Professor Ying Gao, I used these tools to study how platforms like YouTube should decide how long to try out new creators. My thesis applies them to AI. I treat the developer and the AI system as two players, and work out how often, and in what form, developers should run surprise tests so that behaving well at all times becomes the AI’s best strategy. 

Next, I plan to complete a Master's in Computer Science at UBC and then a PhD in economics, with the goal of becoming a professor or AI researcher. This award would let me focus on research and pay for the computing power my thesis requires. AI systems are quickly taking on work that affects all of us, from answering medical questions to writing the software our banks and hospitals run on. Making sure we can trust them is one of the most important problems we face, and it's a problem I want to spend my life tackling.
